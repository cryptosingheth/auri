Auto-invest (DCA)

An Auto-Invest plan is a standing instruction: Auri buys a fixed amount of money's worth of gold every day, week or month at whatever the live price is at that moment, and reports the whole plan back to you as a single average cost per ounce. It takes about a minute to set up, there is no setup fee, and you can pause, resize or stop it whenever you want.

Why people do this

Buying gold in one go means making one price decision, and most people make that decision badly — they buy after a rally and stop after a fall. A recurring plan removes the decision: the money is fixed, the timing is fixed, and only the number of ounces you receive varies. It is a budgeting and behaviour tool first and a pricing tool second. Read About dollar-cost averaging below before you commit to a monthly amount.

Before you start

  • An Auri account. See Start here.
  • At least $10 per cycle. The app rejects anything smaller with "Enter at least $10 per cycle".
  • A funding source you can sustain for months, not weeks: your cash balance, a linked bank account, or a per-cycle top-up reminder.
  • If you fund from a bank account, the one-time identity check has to be complete first — bank rails require it, and crypto funding does not. See Start here.
  • An understanding that gold pays no interest, no dividend and no rent. A plan accumulates metal, not income.

About 1 minute to set up · 0.49% Auri fee on each buy, setup fee free, network fee covered · each individual buy is irreversible once it executes, but the plan itself is cancellable at any time.

Create a recurring plan

  1. Open Auto-Invest. Reach it from the main navigation, from the Auto-Invest card on Home, or from the Actions catalogue. The page opens on your existing plans with an inline form for a new one.
  2. Enter the amount per cycle. Minimum $10. This is an amount of money, not an amount of gold — that is the entire mechanic. When gold is expensive the same $200 buys fewer ounces; when it is cheap it buys more.
  3. Choose the frequency. Daily, Weekly or Monthly. That is the complete list — there is no payday option and no custom interval. The schedule anchors to the moment you start the plan, so if you start a weekly plan on the day you get paid, every later buy lands on that same weekday.
  4. Pick where the money comes from. Three options: Cash balance (auto-debited from your USDC cash each cycle — the most reliable, because nothing external has to succeed); Bank account (auto-debited each cycle over local bank rails — EFT in Canada, bank transfer in the US); or a top-up reminder, where Auri does not pull anything and instead reminds you each cycle to fund the buy yourself (in Canada that top-up is Interac e-Transfer). The reminder option is the only one that will not execute on its own.
  5. Check the review screen. It shows Each investment, Frequency, In gold (an approximate ounce figure at the current price), Funded from, and Setup fee: Free. The ounce figure is an estimate at today's price, not a commitment — the actual ounces are set by the live price at each execution.
  6. Confirm. The plan starts immediately with its first buy. Confirmation reads "Auto-Invest started — Auri will buy $X of gold weekly, starting now. Manage, pause or stop it anytime."

What you'll see when it works

Each plan appears as its own card showing the amount, the cadence, its status, the next buy and last buy dates, gold accumulated, invested to date, number of buys, average price paid, current worth, and return in both dollars and percent. The page header aggregates the same figures across every plan you run, including a blended average cost.

The number to sanity-check after the second cycle is buys count. If it has not incremented on schedule, the plan did not execute — check the funding source before anything else. Gold bought by a plan is ordinary vault gold: it is free to sell, gift or send, and it earns 2× Auri Jewels instead of the standard 1 Jewel per $1 bought. Rates are on the reference page.

Pause, edit, resume and stop

  • Pause keeps the plan and its history intact and stops future buys. Nothing is sold. This is also how you skip a single cycle — there is no dedicated skip control, so you pause, let the date pass, and resume.
  • Resume restarts the cadence from the moment you resume, not from where it left off.
  • Edit changes the amount and the frequency in place. Your accumulated gold, buys count and average cost carry over, so a plan you resize keeps reporting one blended average across both the old and the new amount. If you want a clean before-and-after record, stop the plan and start a second one instead.
  • Stop ends the plan permanently. It does not sell anything — the gold stays in your vault and simply stops growing.

How a plan reports average cost and P&L

Average cost is straightforward arithmetic: total invested ÷ total ounces the plan has bought. Because each buy spends the same money at a different price, that average is not the simple average of the prices you paid — it is pulled below it, because a fixed amount buys more ounces at low prices than at high ones.

Worked example, four weekly buys of $200. The prices are illustrative, not a forecast; your app uses the live price.

The plan reports an average cost of $4,108/oz — that is $800 ÷ 0.1947 oz. Strip the 0.49% fee out and the pure averaging effect got you gold at $4,088/oz against a simple price average of $4,100/oz. That $12 gap is the whole of what averaging bought you over four cycles. It is real, and it is small.

Three things the reported return does not tell you:

  • It is unrealised and pre-cost. Selling costs another 0.49%, and any tax consequence is yours. See fees and limits.
  • The percentage is not comparable to a lump-sum return. Your first dollar has been invested for months and your most recent dollar for days. A plan showing "+7%" has not earned 7% on all of your money for the whole period.
  • Average cost is not a floor. It is a record of what you paid. The price can and does sit below it for long stretches.

What can go wrong

  • "My plan didn't buy this week." Check the funding source on the plan card. A top-up reminder plan never executes on its own — it waits for you to fund it.
  • "I set the minimum and it wouldn't save." The floor is $10 per cycle, checked before the review step.
  • "My average cost went up after I edited the plan." Editing blends the new amount into the existing history rather than starting fresh. Stop and start a new plan if you need separate records.
  • "I can't sell the gold my plan bought." If you have an open loan, some of your gold is locked as collateral and cannot be sold, gifted or sent until you repay or unlock it. Plan buys add to your total, and borrowing against that total locks it. See Borrow against gold.
  • A failed bank collection. The app does not currently state what happens when a scheduled bank debit is declined — whether it retries, skips, or pauses the plan. Treat this as UNKNOWN and watch the buys count until it is documented.

About dollar-cost averaging

What it does

Dollar-cost averaging means investing equal amounts at regular intervals regardless of the price. Mechanically it does exactly two things. It makes your ounces-per-dollar vary inversely with price, so a fixed budget buys more when gold is cheap. And it takes the timing decision away from you, which for most people is the larger effect by a wide margin.

What it does not do

It does not guarantee a profit. It does not protect you against loss. In a sustained decline, a plan buys the whole way down and you end up holding more ounces at a lower price than you started with — averaging reduces how badly a single bad entry hurts, it does not remove the exposure. Anyone describing DCA as a risk-management technique is overselling it: it manages regret, not risk.

Rising and falling markets, on identical numbers

Four $200 buys, $800 total, against putting the whole $800 in on day one.

Falling market — $4,400, $4,100, $3,800, $3,500. The plan accumulates 0.2030 oz at an average cost of $3,941/oz, worth $711 at the end: down $89, or 11%. The lump sum bought 0.1809 oz at $4,400, worth $633: down $167, or 21%. Averaging lost less. It still lost.

Rising market — the same four prices in the opposite order: $3,800, $4,000, $4,200, $4,400. The plan accumulates 0.1947 oz at $4,108/oz, worth $857: up $57, or 7%. The lump sum bought 0.2095 oz at $3,800, worth $922: up $122, or 15%. Averaging earned less.

That is the honest trade, and it is symmetric: spreading purchases in narrows the range of outcomes in both directions. Historically, investing a lump sum immediately has more often beaten spreading it in, because markets rise more often than they fall. People still choose to spread in, and reasonably so — a smaller worst case is worth paying for if the worst case is what would make you abandon the position.

Why averaging suits a volatile asset, and payday alignment

The averaging benefit grows with the dispersion of prices you buy at. Gold moves several percent in a week fairly often, which is precisely the condition under which a fixed-dollar schedule collects meaningfully different prices. On something that barely moves, DCA is pointless overhead.

Cadence matters far less than sustainability. Weekly versus monthly makes almost no difference to your average cost over years, and none at all to your fee. What matters is whether the amount survives a bad month. A $100 monthly plan you keep for five years beats a $500 monthly plan you cancel in month four, every time. Since the schedule anchors to when you start it, starting on payday keeps every debit landing while the money is there — the app has no payday setting, so you create the alignment by choosing your start day.

The projection calculator, and why it is illustrative only

The Auto-Invest page includes a projection calculator: enter an amount, a cadence and a number of years and it compounds your contributions forward. It applies a hard-coded 8.5% per year, described in the app as gold's long-run average over roughly the last two decades, and it carries its own disclaimer that it "assumes a constant 8.5%/yr and excludes fees and taxes".

Treat that output as arithmetic, not a forecast. A constant annual rate is not how any asset behaves: real gold has multi-year stretches of decline, and for a stream of contributions the order of returns changes the ending value even when the average is identical. The chosen 8.5% is also a backward-looking figure drawn from one particular window; a different window gives a different answer. Past performance does not predict future returns, and no projection on this page or in the app is a promise of any outcome.

Use the calculator for the one thing it is actually good at: seeing how much you will have contributed. That number is certain. The growth on top of it is not.

When not to start a plan

  • You are carrying high-interest debt. A credit-card balance is a guaranteed cost. Gold is not a guaranteed return.
  • You will need the money within twelve months. Gold can be down when your deadline arrives, and selling costs 0.49% on the way out.
  • You are starting because the price just jumped. That is the timing decision the plan is supposed to remove, made once, badly, at the setup screen.
  • The per-cycle amount is one you would have to stop. Halve it. A plan you keep is the only kind that averages anything.
  • You are running a leveraged Boost position. Averaging in slowly and holding borrowed exposure work against each other. See Boost and Risks.
